Why Is My Water Bill So High? Common Causes of Unexplained Water Loss.

A sudden or gradual jump in your water bill with no obvious explanation usually has a real, findable cause, and a few checks can often point you straight to it.

By the Brea Apex Plumbing team · 2026-09-22

If your water bill has climbed and you can't point to an obvious reason, like new landscaping or a house full of guests, there's a good chance water is escaping somewhere you can't see it. Here's how to narrow down where before calling for a full diagnostic. A little detective work before calling for a full leak detection service can sometimes point you straight to the answer yourself.

Check Your Water Meter First.

With every fixture and appliance in the house off, look at your water meter's leak indicator, usually a small triangular or circular dial that spins if any water is flowing. If it's moving with everything off, water is escaping somewhere in your system, confirming a real leak exists before you spend money finding it.

The Usual Suspects.

A running toilet is one of the most common and most overlooked causes, since it can waste water silently if the flapper isn't sealing completely. A dripping faucet adds up over a billing cycle more than most people expect. An irrigation system leak, especially underground, can run for weeks before it's noticed. And a hidden pipe leak inside a wall or under a slab can be the largest single cause, since it often runs continuously and undetected.

How to Narrow It Down Yourself.

Put a few drops of food coloring in the toilet tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing, if color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. Check faucets for drips even when fully closed. If your irrigation system runs on a timer, check for unusually wet or soggy patches of lawn on days it wasn't scheduled to run.

When It's Time to Call for Leak Detection.

If the meter test confirms a leak but you can't find an obvious source (no running toilet, no dripping faucet, no wet irrigation area), that points to a hidden leak, behind a wall, under a floor, or under the slab. That's when acoustic and electronic leak detection equipment earns its cost, rather than guessing and opening walls.

How to Read Your Bill for Clues.

Most water utilities show usage in consumption units over a billing period. Comparing your last several bills side by side can reveal whether an increase was sudden (pointing to a specific event, like a new leak starting) or gradual (more consistent with slow degradation, like aging pipe or a slowly worsening toilet flapper). A sudden jump with no change in household habits is the pattern most worth investigating promptly.

What If You Rent Rather Than Own.

If you're a renter noticing a high water bill, the same checks apply, but you'll want to loop in your landlord or property manager before any repair, since responsibility for the fix (and sometimes the bill itself) depends on your lease terms. Documenting what you found with the checks above gives your landlord useful, specific information rather than a vague complaint.

Seasonal Water Use Changes to Rule Out First.

Before assuming a leak, consider whether anything seasonal explains the jump: increased irrigation as weather warms, more laundry from house guests, or filling a pool for the first time in a while. These are legitimate, non-leak explanations for a higher bill, and ruling them out first saves you from an unnecessary leak-detection call for something that was actually just increased normal usage.

What a Sudden Spike Usually Means Versus a Gradual Climb.

A sudden, dramatic spike in a single billing period often points to a specific new event, a pipe that started leaking, a toilet flapper that just failed, or a fixture left running. A gradual climb over several billing cycles is more consistent with a slowly worsening issue, aging pipe, a softener malfunctioning, or a slow-growing leak. Which pattern you're seeing helps narrow down what to check first.

Quick Reference.

Self-Check Before You Call.

  • Test the meter's leak indicator with everything off.
  • Food-coloring test every toilet in the house.
  • Check all faucets for drips when fully closed.
  • Walk your yard for unexpected wet or soggy patches.
  • Compare your last 3-6 months of water bills for the pattern (sudden jump vs. gradual climb).

FAQs.

How much water can a running toilet actually waste?
A continuously running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons a day depending on the severity, which shows up as a real, sometimes dramatic bill increase over a billing cycle.
What if the meter test shows no movement but my bill is still high?
That suggests the cause is usage-based rather than a leak, worth checking irrigation scheduling, appliance changes, or household size changes before assuming a hidden leak.
Can a water softener affect my bill?
A softener uses some water during its regeneration cycle, which is normal and typically small, not enough to explain a significant bill increase on its own.
Can the water utility itself help me figure out if I have a leak?
Some utilities offer usage alerts or historical data that can help spot an unusual pattern, worth checking your provider's online account tools alongside the checks in this guide.
