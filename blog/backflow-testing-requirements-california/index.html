A Homeowner's Guide to Backflow Testing Requirements in California.

If your property has an irrigation system, you likely have a legal testing requirement you may not know about, and staying current is easier than most homeowners assume.

By the Brea Apex Plumbing team · 2026-09-22

Backflow prevention devices protect the public water supply from contamination flowing backward from a private irrigation or plumbing system. California water providers generally require annual testing for properties with these devices, and skipping it can eventually affect your water service. This applies whether you've lived in your home for years or just moved in and inherited a system you don't know much about.

What a Backflow Device Actually Does.

Under certain pressure conditions, water can theoretically flow backward from your property's irrigation system or plumbing into the public water main, potentially carrying fertilizer, pesticides, or other contaminants with it. A backflow prevention device is a mechanical valve that stops that reverse flow, protecting the shared water supply for the whole neighborhood, not just your property.

Who Actually Needs One.

Most residential irrigation systems in this area require a backflow device by code. Certain commercial connections and any property with an auxiliary water source also typically require one. A standard home with no irrigation system and no unusual water connections usually doesn't need one.

Why Annual Testing Is Required.

Backflow devices are mechanical and can fail over time, a worn seal or stuck valve can compromise the protection it's supposed to provide. Water providers require annual testing specifically because a device's condition isn't visually obvious, testing is the only reliable way to confirm it's actually working.

What Happens If You Skip It.

Water providers track compliance and typically send a testing reminder notice. If testing lapses long enough, some providers can restrict or shut off water service until compliance is restored, which is a bigger hassle than the test itself would have been.

What Test Results Actually Look Like.

A passing test confirms the device holds pressure correctly and prevents reverse flow under the tested conditions, documented on a standardized form submitted to your water provider. A failing test means the device needs repair or replacement before it's compliant again, and we can typically handle that in the same visit to avoid a return trip and resubmit passing results promptly.

What If You're Buying or Selling a Home With a Backflow Device.

A backflow device's testing compliance status is worth checking during a real estate transaction, an out-of-compliance device is a straightforward, inexpensive thing to resolve before closing rather than something a buyer discovers as a surprise. We can test and document current status if this comes up during a sale.

Do New Installations Need Immediate Testing.

Yes, a newly installed backflow device typically needs an initial test at installation to confirm it's functioning correctly before the annual testing cycle begins. If you've recently added an irrigation system, don't assume you're covered until that initial test and documentation are complete.

Who Performs the Test.

Backflow testing must be done by a certified tester using calibrated test equipment, it's not something a homeowner can self-certify even if they understand the mechanics. We handle both the testing and the required documentation submission, so you don't need to separately track down a certified tester and then figure out how to file the paperwork with your water provider. The whole process, from scheduling to documentation, is typically handled in a single visit, start to finish.

Quick Reference.

Backflow Testing Basics.

  • Check whether your property has an irrigation system, that's the most common trigger for a testing requirement.
  • Look for a backflow device near your irrigation valve or main shutoff, it's a visible above-ground assembly.
  • Respond promptly to any testing notice from your water provider.
  • Keep documentation of passing test results in case of a compliance question.
  • If a device fails testing, have it repaired or replaced promptly rather than letting compliance lapse.
  • Keep your certified tester's contact information on hand for next year's renewal.
  • Ask whether your device is single-check or double-check type, testing requirements can vary slightly by type.
  • Ask us to add a reminder for next year's test when we complete this year's, so it's one less thing to track yourself.

FAQs.

How much does backflow testing cost?
Typically $100-$175 per device, including the documentation submitted to your water provider.
What if I just moved into a home and don't know if I have a backflow device?
Check near your irrigation valve or main shutoff for a visible above-ground assembly, or ask us to check during any service visit.
Can a backflow device be installed indoors?
Most residential devices are installed outdoors near the irrigation valve for accessibility, though placement can vary by property layout and local code requirements. Indoor installation shows up occasionally in commercial settings where outdoor placement isn't practical, but it's uncommon for a typical residential irrigation setup.
Do all backflow devices eventually need to be replaced, even with regular testing?
Like any mechanical device, yes, eventually. Regular testing extends useful life by catching problems early, but a device that's failed testing multiple times is usually more cost-effective to replace than keep repairing.
